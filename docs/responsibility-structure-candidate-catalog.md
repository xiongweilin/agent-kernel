# Responsibility Structure Candidate Catalog

> **Knowledge and evidence boundary:** This document preserves **structure candidates** that can repeatedly be proposed, compared, and tested across multiple domains. Structural similarity provides only an entry point for generating questions and comparisons; it does not automatically create cross-domain identity of mechanisms, predictive strength, causal conclusions, value judgments, or intervention effectiveness. Concrete objects, mechanisms, thresholds, statistical relations, professional standards, and real-world consequences must return to domain models, external evidence, and situational facts for testing.
>
> **Terminology namespace:** “Structure candidate” here is an epistemic structure candidate. It does not correspond to the `candidate` lifecycle state of a rule or object in the [Responsibility Record Plane](09-responsibility-record-plane.md).

## Definition Dependencies and Migration Rules

The V1 public interface of this document includes only **structure candidate** and **cross-domain migration type**. The relational, change, epistemic, and governance entries below form a dynamic catalog: they may be rigorously described and tested, but inclusion here does not grant them a platform-level canonical ID. Meta-model concepts are inherited from the [Theory of Determinative Responsibility](02-theory-of-determinative-responsibility.md), intelligence mechanisms from the [Theory of Cognitive Responsibility](03-theory-of-cognitive-responsibility.md), and action procedures from [Action Responsibility Practice](08-action-responsibility-practice.md).

Each entry uses four columns: **Candidate, Minimum definition, Core question, Boundary**. The candidate catalog is not part of the V1 frozen surface. Persistent counterexamples, changes in scale, proliferating exceptions, or new objects may trigger addition, narrowing, splitting, renaming, or deletion without thereby changing Framework V1.0. Concrete mechanisms, thresholds, and facts always belong to domain models.

Migration types are limited to:

- **Common problem:** migrate a way of asking questions;
- **Formal similarity:** migrate a mode of representation or comparison;
- **Mechanism similarity:** only when object, boundary conditions, and mechanism evidence are sufficiently close may limited predictive or intervention experience be migrated.

The same candidate may have different migration types in different domains. Mechanism evidence in one domain must not be automatically copied to another.

Therefore:

$$
\boxed{
\text{candidate schema / migration rule frozen}
\quad\land\quad
\text{candidate catalog dynamic}
}
$$

## I. Relational-Form Candidates

| Candidate | Minimum definition | Core question | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Object–environment boundary | Analysis must provisionally determine the object, components, environment, and boundary; changing the boundary may change variables, causal attribution, responsibility, and outcome attribution. | What is inside the system and what is externalized? Does the conclusion survive redrawing the boundary? | A boundary is not the ultimate partition of reality; reopen under persistent cross-boundary effects, responsibility mismatch, or scale conflict. |
| State–transition | At a given scale and time, an object may have distinguishable states; change may appear as retention, gradual variation, transition, generation, disappearance, or relational reorganization. | Which state differences matter? What conditions change state? Is change reversible, irreversible, or hysteretic? | Static classification is not a dynamic mechanism; temporal succession is not causality. Reconstruct when classification persistently depends on exceptions. |
| Distinction–factor–classification mapping | Through distinction, a finite system retains some real differences as factors and then forms comparable structures through names, types, metrics, or relations. | Which differences are merged or separated? Why is the granularity proportionate? What are the consequences of misclassification? | Classification is not an ultimate inventory of reality; anomalies, boundary cases, or scale conflicts require redrawing. |
| Cross-context identity–correspondence retention | The same name, field, dimension, or formal position does not automatically preserve identity of objects, factors, indicators, or roles across contexts; comparison requires standards for what may change and what must remain. | Under what retention standard may acquisitions in two contexts be treated as identical, equivalent, comparable, or transferable? Does the correspondence survive structural change? | Correspondence is not conferred by naming and is not permanent identity; concrete retention mechanisms and evidence belong to domain models. |
| Layer–scale | The same object may exhibit different effective structures at different spatial, temporal, organizational, or relational scales; local stability may not extend to the whole. | What does aggregation preserve or lose? Does a local gain depend on consumption at another layer? | Higher-level description is not a term-by-term copy of lower-level mechanisms; lower-level explanation does not automatically eliminate an effective higher-level description. |
| Sequence–dependency network | Change has order, duration, concurrency, cycles, and prerequisites; some steps can occur only after conditions are met. | Where are prerequisites, concurrent effects, accumulated history, and irreversible points? | Earlier occurrence does not imply causation, and process order does not imply normative necessity. |
| Reachable states–prerequisites–option space | Future states depend not only on current capability or resources but also on prerequisite states, qualifications, interfaces, relations, records, resources, historical conditions, and timing conditions that must be formed in advance or continuously maintained. Current actions may open, preserve, reduce the cost of, or close future paths. | What prerequisites must be acquired in advance for future states? Which can be restored later, and which have timing windows, hysteresis, or unrecoverable loss? What does preserving a path cost to maintain, and how does abandoning it change later reachability? | More options are not automatically better; preserving options itself consumes resources and opportunity. Concrete qualifications, time windows, entry probabilities, switching costs, and reachability belong to domain models and situational facts. |
| Input–process–output–consequence | A system acquires input differences through channels and forms outputs through internal processes; real consequences after outputs enter the environment require separate observation. | How are inputs obtained? How do internal states mediate? What did the output actually change? | Output is not outcome, and outcome cannot completely reconstruct process; reconstruct when consequences diverge from output metrics. |
| Real condition–resource status–constraint–cost | A condition acquires functional status as resource, obstacle, risk, or background only relative to the system, orientation, capability, accessibility, mode of use, time, and environment. | Who can acquire and use the condition? What goal does it support? Who bears maintenance and exit costs? | A condition is not inherently a resource; more resources do not guarantee a larger feasible domain. Rejudge when capability or dependency changes. |
| Feedback–regulation–correction | Feedback is a result entering a later process; regulation is feedback actually changing the process; correction modifies distinction, structure, judgment, action, or conditions under a standard or purpose. | Through what channel does feedback travel? What is the delay? Who can receive and act? Is the standard valid? | Feedback does not imply correction, and metric recovery does not imply that the real problem is solved. |

## II. Change-Mechanism Candidates

| Candidate | Minimum definition | Core question | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reinforcing and balancing loops | Accumulating feedback forms when outcomes continuously change the next round's conditions; reinforcing loops amplify an existing direction while balancing loops constrain deviation. | What are the nodes, directions, periods, delays, gains, thresholds, saturation, external costs, and interruption points? | Reinforcing is not beneficial and balancing is not healthy; one instance of feedback is not a loop. |
| Expansion–maintenance–regeneration | Capabilities, assets, tools, division of labor, or institutions may enlarge the current feasible domain while increasing burdens of maintenance, handoff, recovery, and reorganization. | Who maintains added capability? Does it crowd out buffer and exploration? Are exit and succession feasible? | Larger scale is not greater net capability; low current cost is not low lifecycle cost. |
| Dynamic viable balance | Under an explicit boundary, environment, and time scale, key variables remain in an evidence-supported viable region and can still be detected, regulated, repaired, or reorganized after deviation. | What are the key variables and time windows? Is detection–judgment–action faster than irrecoverable damage? | Current stability is not recoverability and still less normative worthiness of persistence; viability ranges require domain evidence. |
| Higher-order judgment and reflexive loop | Subjects may judge objects and others' judgments; propagated through power and action, these higher-order judgments feed back to change others and reality. | What are object error, higher-order judgment error, and action-coupling error respectively? | Group judgment is not a simple average of member opinions; predicting others does not confer legitimate authority to control them. |
| Path dependence and lock-in | Early choices alter later costs, benefit conditions, and available paths, giving some directions cumulative advantage and potentially making switching progressively harder. | Which capabilities, assets, qualifications, relations, recognizable records, habits, standards, or interests alter later benefits, entry costs, and paths through historical accumulation? Which accumulations are reversible, substitutable, or reacquirable? | Historical persistence is not the same mechanism as copying, heredity, learning, compounding, or institutional continuity. Do not explain all accumulation by one mechanism; reevaluate when alternative paths or environment change. |
| Bottleneck and limiting factor | Under an explicit system, purpose, and scale, overall capacity may be limited by a small number of scarce or slow links. | Does adding non-bottleneck resources have little effect? Where does the next bottleneck appear after the current constraint is relieved? | One failure does not prove a bottleneck, and a bottleneck model is not a complete list of constraints. |
| Structural sensitivity | Small changes at some positions can alter many downstream states and paths; authority, routing, defaults, evaluators, thresholds, and boundaries are often sensitive. | How many paths does a unit change affect? Does it close future options? Can it be detected, rolled back, or externally terminated? | Small observed magnitude does not imply small governance impact; a sensitive node need not be a bottleneck. |
| Threshold, nonlinearity, and hysteresis | Inputs and outcomes may be non-proportional; behavior may change rapidly after thresholds, and recovery paths may differ from onset paths. | How is the threshold estimated? Are there saturation, congestion, abrupt transition, hysteresis, or cumulative damage? | Thresholds cannot be invented from a few cases; state recovery does not erase historical cost. |
| Diffusion and network effects | Information, behavior, disease, resources, and risks may propagate along connections, with speed and reach depending on network structure and node differences. | What are the key links, bridge nodes, blocking conditions, feedback, and externalities? | Node properties alone do not determine propagation; “diffusion” in different domains does not imply the same mechanism. |
| Resource competition and substitution | Multiple processes sharing limited conditions crowd one another; some resources are substitutable, but substitution rates and side effects depend on domain conditions. | What is scarce? Who bears opportunity cost? Are there non-substitutable or non-compensable boundaries? | Do not assume every cost can be compensated by one currency, utility, or total score. |
| Local optimization and global deterioration | Optimizing a local metric may transfer costs to other components, future stages, or external subjects and worsen the whole-system result. | Who defines the system boundary? Are local goals compatible with whole-system consequences? To whom are costs transferred? | Improvement in a local metric does not automatically mean achievement of the overall purpose. |
| Coupling, redundancy, and recovery | Tight coupling can speed coordination while amplifying fault propagation; redundancy adds cost but can support substitution, takeover, experimentation, and recovery. | Where are single points of dependency? Are isolation, buffering, rollback, takeover, and graceful degradation available? | Not all duplication is waste; absence of failure is not recovery capability. |

## III. Epistemic-Structure Candidates

| Candidate | Minimum definition | Core question | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Material acquisition–distinction–evidential force | How connection to reality obtains inputs, how a finite system turns some of them into factors through distinction, and what support, weakening, discrimination, or unresolved force those acquisitions have on candidate structures are three separable but interacting epistemic responsibility positions. | Does the present difference arise because different inputs were acquired, because similar inputs were distinguished differently, or because acquired content received different evidential-force judgments? | More material does not mean better distinction; stable distinction does not mean unbiased input. The same acquisition can support different distinctions and similar distinctions can come from different acquisitions; neither material nor distinction decides the evidential conclusion by itself. See [Theory of Cognitive Responsibility](03-theory-of-cognitive-responsibility.md); hand machine recording to the [Responsibility Record Plane](09-responsibility-record-plane.md). |
| Signal–noise and error tradeoff | Greater sensitivity may increase false positives; greater specificity may increase false negatives. Thresholds must be tied to object, purpose, and consequences of errors. | Which errors matter more? How does the threshold change false-negative and false-positive structure? | Concrete thresholds cannot be copied directly across domains. |
| Actual state–observable representation–admission judgment | An object's actual state or capability, representations others can acquire and recognize, and admission, authorization, resource-allocation, or trust judgments based on those representations should remain separate. Real state is not guaranteed to be visible, and observable representation is not guaranteed to be true. | What can the decision maker actually observe? Which records, indicators, certifications, works, reputations, tests, or relations serve as proxies? Can changes in true state enter those representations and alter admission? What omissions, misidentifications, manipulation, or lock-in can proxies cause? | Representation is not true state, and true state does not automatically obtain social or institutional admission. Proxy mechanisms, admission rules, discrimination risks, legal boundaries, and validity belong to domain models and norms. |
| Correlation–causality separation | Covariation supplies only correlational evidence; causal claims require proportionate procedures for direction, mechanism, alternatives, counterfactuals, or intervention. | What common causes, reverse causality, or selection mechanisms exist? What evidence can discriminate them? | Concrete causal standards belong to domain models and mathematical/statistical methods. |
| Model–reality and blind spots in shared representation | A model may be effective but incomplete. Multiple models sharing data sources, object boundaries, classifications, variables, representations, or background assumptions may omit the same differences together. | Which omissions are controlled, uncontrolled, or unknown? Are different evidence sources genuinely independent? | Internal consistency does not prove fit to reality; repeated use of the same premise does not count as multiple independent pieces of evidence. |
| Sample–population | Local cases support population conclusions only when sampling method, distribution stability, and object similarity are stated. | How did samples enter? Do averages hide subgroups, extremes, or distribution shifts? | Local stability and population stability do not automatically imply one another. |
| Positive cases–counterexamples–boundary cases | Positive cases show what a current structure explains; counterexamples show what it does not; boundary cases test classification and applicability. | What observation or intervention would make competing structures generate different expectations? What would lower confidence in the current preferred structure? | Accumulating supportive material alone does not create discriminative evidence; see open validation in [Theory of Cognitive Responsibility](03-theory-of-cognitive-responsibility.md). |
| Within-model unknown–incomplete candidate space | The former means objects, variables, states, and relations are specified but evidence is insufficient; the latter means those specifications themselves may omit something. | Is the unknown merely an unknown parameter/state, or must object types, variables, scale, or boundary be redrawn? | Important unknowns must not be silently swallowed by zero values, defaults, or an “other” category. |
| Local determination–joint compatibility–joint determination | Local determinations can hold separately without necessarily entering one joint structure; even when compatible, they need not sufficiently determine joint organizational information. | Is each local determination clear? Can they be jointly retained? If compatible, does a joint structure remain underdetermined by the local representations? | Distinguish undefined, incompatible, and underdetermined. Do not infer a complete Cartesian product or global common state merely because individual variables exist. |
| Prediction–post hoc explanation | Prediction constrains candidate outcomes and conditions before the result; post hoc explanation organizes reasons after the result is known and can more easily absorb evidence selectively. | What ex ante discriminating expectations does the current structure generate? Does anomalous success suggest a simpler structure? | Matching a prediction does not prove an explanation sufficient; successful outcomes can also trigger reopening. |
| Problem–credibility–consequence propagation | A problem, credibility of an answer, action priority, and normative legitimacy have different structures; each step through which knowledge enters implementation, adoption, and institutional embedding has independent conditions. | What are the object and scope, unknowns, candidate answers, evidence, solution criteria, and reopening conditions? | Credibility, priority, and legitimacy must not be collapsed into one number. |
| Persistent residual–structural tension | When current models, classifications, goals, validation, or action arrangements persistently leave residuals not adequately absorbed, structural tension may be proposed. | Which phenomena continually require patches? Which internal structures or goals can no longer be jointly maintained? | One anomaly does not prove structural error; mechanism definition belongs to [Theory of Cognitive Responsibility](03-theory-of-cognitive-responsibility.md). |
| Instrumental intervention–epistemic intervention | Action may primarily alter a target state, or primarily obtain real differences that discriminate candidates, reduce key unknowns, or expose failure conditions. | What lowest-cost observation, experiment, pilot, or minimal reproduction would make candidates generate different expectations? | Epistemic intervention is not purposeless trial and error; more information does not necessarily have more decision value. |
| Revision depth–attribution layer | After an anomalous result, the thing requiring revision may be execution, judgment, structure, factors, object boundary, goals, validation method, or self-model. | Which layer does current evidence most support changing? What would show that the revision was too shallow or too deep? | Deeper revision is not inherently superior; mechanism is in [Theory of Cognitive Responsibility](03-theory-of-cognitive-responsibility.md), procedure in [Action Responsibility Practice](08-action-responsibility-practice.md). |
| Positive knowledge–negative knowledge–bounded transfer | Experience may preserve both what works and what fails under which conditions, which paths are costly, and which boundary changes should stop further migration. | Which structures should be called long-term, which only within the current scope? Which failures and reopening conditions are worth preserving? | Failure experience is not a universal prohibition, and successful migration is not permission for unbounded expansion. |
| Proportionate closure–candidate formation–solving–reopen pressure | Tasks can be compared by the current difficulty of reaching proportionate closure, the difficulty of forming candidates and solving under closure, and the pressure from advancing reality that makes current closure mismatched again; closure should also distinguish epistemic and real-action purposes. | Does current difficulty mainly come from failure to reach proportionate closure, failure to form good enough candidates, or difficulty solving under an existing representation? Is current knowledge enough to continue solving; even if so, are the conditions for real intervention met? Is new reality asking for a different answer, a different search strategy, or a redefinition of the problem? | These quantities are working projections of task state, not intrinsic domain properties; they need not be measurable and do not form one total difficulty function. $Z_t$ is dynamic mismatch pressure and is not presupposed to be a symmetric, independent static coordinate alongside $X,Y$. |

**Proportionate closure–candidate formation–solving–reopen pressure (working representation)**

$$
X=(X_E,X_A),\qquad X_E=\text{difficulty of reaching proportionate epistemic closure},\qquad X_A=\text{difficulty of reaching closure sufficient to support real-world action}
$$

$X_A$ is not merely “a little more epistemic work”; the gap may arise from authority, third-party rights, irreversibility, recovery conditions, and legitimacy.

$$
Y=(Y_g,Y_s),\qquad Y_g=\text{difficulty of forming valuable candidates under current closure},\qquad Y_s=\text{difficulty of comparing, computing, implementing, or solving under the current representation and candidates}
$$

$$
\boxed{\text{do not assume commensurability, additivity, or monotonic correlation}}
$$

$$
Z_t=\text{reopen pressure applied by advancing reality to the current closure}
$$

**Core questions:**

- Does current difficulty mainly come from not yet reaching proportionate closure, not forming good enough candidates, or solving difficulty under the existing representation?
- Is current knowledge already sufficient to continue solving? Even if so, are the conditions for real intervention met?
- Is new reality asking us to revise the answer, the search strategy, or the problem definition?

**Boundary:** These quantities are working projections of task state, not intrinsic properties of a domain. They need not be numerically measurable and do not form a unified total-difficulty function. The same task may move over time; the same domain may contain tasks in entirely different positions. $Z_t$ is dynamic mismatch pressure and is not presupposed to be symmetric with or independent of $X,Y$.

## IV. Governance-Structure Candidates

This section preserves only governance patterns reusable across domains. It does not define concrete approval processes, legal rights, market mechanisms, or engineering thresholds.

| Candidate | Minimum definition | Core question | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cost–control–benefit–responsibility separation | Causal contribution, actual control, authorization status, benefit, risk bearing, and responsibility for repair should be traced separately. | Who can change reality, who benefits, who bears risk and unrecoverable loss, and who is responsible for repair? | Do not collapse these into one “responsible actor,” and do not erase non-compensable loss with aggregate benefit. |
| Distribution of power and substantive role separation | Proposing, observing, interpreting, setting standards, deciding, executing, verifying, handling dissent, revising, renewing, and stopping may be carried by different roles. | Is role separation realized as actual independence in information, resources, action, and stopping capacity? | Different names do not imply substantive independence. |
| Concentration–substitutability–anti-lock-in | Concentration of resources, data, infrastructure, standards, interfaces, market entry, and exit control can improve coordination while increasing lock-in. | Do migration, interoperability, substitution, takeover, and new-entry paths exist in reality? | Concentration is not automatically ineffective or illegitimate; concrete thresholds belong to domains. |
| Shared resources and aggregate boundaries | For shared resources with limited capacity, cumulative damage, or difficult recovery, absolute use, capacity thresholds, allocation rules, and recovery conditions must be checked together. | Is cumulative total use tolerable? How are cost and recovery responsibility distributed? | Unit efficiency, individual payment, or low single-event risk does not automatically prove safe aggregate use. |
| Institutionalized dissent | Dissent should be able to enter formal records, receive traceable responses, and trigger review, restriction, or suspension when predefined conditions are reached. | Who may dissent? How is dissent recorded and answered, and how can it change procedure? | Whether dissent is correct, has procedural standing, or has veto power must be judged separately. |
| Epistemic boundaries and evidence governance | Metrics, models, classifications, and predictions are finite proxies; defining state spaces, reference classes, priors, model sets, loss functions, and action thresholds is itself a governance position. | Who can audit, modify, and stop this epistemic infrastructure? How are shared blind spots detected? | The power to define is power, but not automatically illegitimate power. |
| Reversibility and resilience | The weaker the evidence, larger the impact, tighter the coupling, or harder the recovery, the more exposure should be limited and isolation, buffering, substitution, rollback, takeover, exit, and repair preserved. | After failure, what real recovery paths actually exist? Who bears irrecoverable loss? | “Rollback” must correspond to recoverability of real states, not only restoration of records or software state. |
| Governance recursion | Metrics, standards, authorization, oversight, dissent, exit, and governance rules are themselves institutional objects requiring validation, revision, and retirement. | Who validates governance procedure? Who can modify, stop, or refuse automatic renewal? | A governance procedure cannot prove itself effective by “having completed itself” and must not renew itself by default. |
| Complexity governance | New rules, interfaces, roles, exceptions, and dependencies all create maintenance, validation, and retirement burden. | What is the purpose, owner, maintenance resource, failure mode, and retirement condition of added complexity? | Complexity does not disappear when encapsulated or transferred. |

## V. Entry Points for Subject Relations

When the object of analysis genuinely involves subjects that may have their own experience, boundaries, orientations, and agency, candidate questions include: whether subject boundaries continue to be recognized; whether expression can enter understanding and validation; whether commitments, care, and dependency are visible and renegotiable; whether conflict produces responsibility, repair, and later behavioral change; and whether roles, distance, substitution, and exit are realistically adjustable.

These are only candidate questions. They cannot directly judge whether a particular intimate, family, educational, labor, or human–machine relation is “normal.” The working boundary of subjectivity is defined in the [Theory of Cognitive Responsibility](03-theory-of-cognitive-responsibility.md); concrete relational and institutional mechanisms belong to relevant domain models.

## VI. Cross-Domain Use and Maintenance

When entering an unfamiliar domain:

1. generate candidate questions from entries rather than applying conclusions directly;
2. state the object, boundary, scale, factors, and standards for cross-context correspondence;
3. check whether local determinations are clear, whether they can be combined, and whether local representations are sufficient to support the current joint conclusion;
4. determine whether transfer is a common problem, formal similarity, or mechanism similarity;
5. test with domain evidence and situational facts, distinguishing material acquisition, mode of distinction, and judgment of evidential force;
6. record what must not transfer, together with counterexamples and boundary conditions;
7. narrow, split, rename, or reject candidates where necessary;
8. when candidates enter action, hand off to [Action Responsibility Practice](08-action-responsibility-practice.md); when machine recording is required, use the [Responsibility Record Plane](09-responsibility-record-plane.md).

When combining multiple candidates, distinguish constitutive relations, mechanisms of historical persistence, functional or causal mechanisms, subject relations, power relations, and normative authorization. These arrows must not be assembled into a single historical line by which natural change necessarily becomes intelligence, subjectivity, power, or ethics.

New entries should enter with the weakest appropriate migration type. Only after relatively independent mechanism evidence exists across multiple domains may a specific use be promoted to “mechanism similarity.” Frequent use, model agreement, repetition across documents, or appearance in an official document does not constitute evidence of truth.
